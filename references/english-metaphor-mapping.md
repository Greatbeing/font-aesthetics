# Your Metaphor Notebook — English Edition

## Core Principle

Never grab the surface meaning. Dig for the deep metaphor. The final image should not be an *illustration of the word* but *the word's visual thought*.

One difference from the Chinese notebook matters above all: a Chinese character carries meaning inside its strokes, radicals and components — it can be decomposed, and the decomposition itself is the metaphor. A Latin letter carries no meaning inside itself. It is an abstract shape. So in English, metaphor is built from **form, case, weight, spacing, rhythm and cultural association** — from what happens *to* the letters, not from what is inside them. Never claim a Latin letter "means" something by etymology. Claim it by resemblance, scale, weight, spacing or treatment — and make the treatment visible enough that a viewer who cannot read the definition still feels it.

**A note on typeface names:** AI image models do not set type — they paint it. A named typeface in a prompt is a style hint, not a font selection. The model will approximate high-contrast hairline serifs, not render actual Didot glyphs. Always pair a name with a description of its qualities (see the prompt templates). The faces named below are real, famous typefaces; use them as directions, not as guarantees.

## Metaphor Judgment Process — for Latin Letters

The Chinese process asks what force a word exerts and on what. For Latin words, add one more question — **what happens to the letterform?** — because the letterform is the only body the meaning has.

1. **Ask: what is this word's essential force?** (attract / repel / press / release / dissolve / freeze / rise / fall)
2. **Ask: what does the force act upon?** (space / time / a relationship / the self / order)
3. **Ask: what happens to the letterform under that force?** This is the step the Chinese notebook does not need, and the English one cannot skip. A force that only exists in the background is illustration; a force that bends the letters is metaphor. Examples:
   - Love pulls letters together → ligatures, shared strokes, touching counters.
   - Loneliness separates → wide tracking, each letter alone in space.
   - Struggle presses down → fractured stems, a displaced letter that still stands.
   - Desire stretches upward → elongated ascenders, an S drawn out like a flame.
   - Fear closes in → counters shrinking, apertures shutting, light weight dissolving.
   - Time erodes → the leftmost letters crisp, the rightmost fading, like ink weathered by years.
4. **Find the 1–2 most precise metaphor elements, not a pile of symbols.** One letter treated decisively beats ten decorative objects.
5. **Test it blind:** describe the poster to someone who cannot see it and has not heard the word. If they feel the emotion anyway, the metaphor works. If they only understand it once you name the word, it is still illustration — go deeper.
6. **Check the letter toolkit:** the most pictorial letters are **O** (ring, void, eye, sun), **I** (pillar, self, solitude — and its dot, a small separate soul), **S** (flame, river, spine), **A** (peak, roof), **V** (valley, open arms), **M/W** (mountains, waves), **X** (cross, error, unknown, kiss), **T** (pillar, cross, signpost), **H** (gate, two pillars joined by a bridge). If the word contains one of these, ask first whether *that letter* can carry the metaphor before adding any object.

### Surface vs. Deep — the quick test

| | Surface metaphor | Deep metaphor |
|---|---|---|
| **Source** | The dictionary definition or the first picture that comes to mind | The word's force, its effect on space, time, relationship or self |
| **The letter** | Unchanged — the word is pasted onto an illustration | Transformed — the letterform itself is bent, joined, broken, stretched or spaced by the meaning |
| **The test** | Remove the word and the image still "means" something generic | Remove the word and the image loses its meaning entirely — the word *is* the image |
| **Example** | FREEDOM = a bird flying over the word | FREEDOM = the letters breaking out of a ruled grid, the last letter half outside the frame |

## Emotion → Visual Style Mapping (Latin Typefaces)

The Chinese mapping uses style, space and color. The English mapping adds the fourth column that does the real work: **which typeface class, weight, spacing and case**.

| Emotion | Style preference | Space | Color tendency | Typeface direction |
|---------|-----------------|-------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Tenderness | Poetic negative space, Japanese contemporary plane | Open, breathing | Ivory, warm gray, low saturation | Old Style serif (Garamond, Caslon) Light or Regular, lowercase, generous leading |
| Coldness | Swiss grid, Bauhaus | Precise, restrained | Black and white, cold blue, silver gray | Neo-grotesque (Helvetica, Univers) Regular, even tracking, lowercase or small caps |
| Danger | Constructivism, Expressionism | Oppressive, unstable | Red and black, high contrast | Bold condensed grotesque (Franklin Gothic) or slab (Clarendon), ALL CAPS, tight tracking |
| Loneliness | Extreme minimal negative space, Surrealism | Vast, single center | Large white or black field + one faint warm point | Light weight Humanist sans or Old Style serif, widely tracked, small in the frame |
| Oppression | Monumental, vertical composition | Top-heavy, cramped | Dark tones, heavy metallics | Black weight grotesque or slab, ALL CAPS, letters crowded and pressing down |
| Hope | Light-source, directional | A local light source, pointing far | Dark ground + warm light | Transitional serif (Baskerville) with exaggerated ascenders, or a Geometric sans (Futura) rising on the baseline |
| Poetry | Calligraphic fusion, organic curves | Flowing, light | Faded ink, watercolor texture | Formal script (Snell Roundhand, Bickham Script) for one word only, or Garamond Italic with swashes |
| Absurdity | Surrealism, collage | Contradictory space, irrational | Clashing colors, dissonant pairings | Two mismatched faces colliding — a Didone next to a grotesque, one letter at the wrong size |
| Sublimity | Vertical composition, large scale | Looking up, unreachable | Gold, white, ceremonial color | Trajan capitals, widely tracked, monumental scale |
| Desire | Centripetal, mirrored | Inward, pulling, inescapable | Deep red, dark gold, mirrored reflection | High-contrast Didone (Didot, Bodoni) — stretched vertically, hairlines that could snap |
| Grief | Faded, ghostly, dissolving | Sinking, hollow | Gray, ash, cold blue | Light weight Old Style serif, sinking descenders, the word half-faded into the ground |
| Calm | Minimal plane, generous leading | Still, spacious | Warm white, stone gray | Humanist sans (Gill Sans, Frutiger) Regular, open apertures, generous tracking |
| Urgency | Poster shout, condensed grotesque | Crowded, pressing forward | Black on yellow, red on white | Franklin Gothic Extra Condensed, ALL CAPS, tight tracking, no ornament |
| Eternity | Inscriptional, monumental | Centered, symmetrical, still | Stone, marble, gold on black | Trajan capitals, widely tracked, centered like a monument |

### Case and weight as emotion — the short rules

- **ALL CAPS** is stone: monument, law, shout, eternity. Use it for words that must be carved, not spoken. Track caps wide for monumentality; track them tight and they become a wall.
- **Lowercase** is a whisper: intimacy, confession, vulnerability, the everyday. Use it for tenderness, grief, smallness.
- **Light weight** is a word barely there: grief, memory, dawn, a voice losing strength. Let it dissolve into the background.
- **Black weight** is a word that cannot be ignored: oppression, certainty, mass, night. Let it crush the frame.
- **Wide tracking** is distance: loneliness, time passing, a voice across a void.
- **Tight tracking** is pressure: crowding, urgency, a whisper in the ear, a secret.
- **One word in two weights** is a word arguing with itself — use it for conflict, doubt, or a word with two faces.
